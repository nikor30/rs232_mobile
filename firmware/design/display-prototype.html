RS232 Console display

A new start for the 2-inch touch display. Touch it: the screen below is drawn at the display's real 320 × 240 size, with data that behaves like a switch on the port.

Four moves

  • Swipe sidewaysThe next port. Each port is its own screen, and the dots count them.
  • Pull upDo something: baud, auto-baud, BREAK, record, scripts, USB stick, settings. Tapping the handle does the same.
  • Pull downGet a phone connected: QR code for the hotspot, addresses, Bluetooth PIN. Tapping the top line does the same.
  • Tap the traceThe terminal, with the keys you need when a switch is waiting for one.

Decisions in it

The trace is the screen. Received bytes rise in green, sent bytes fall in blue, one bar per tenth of a second. It moves in steps, so it reads the same at 10 frames per second as at 60. A flat line is an answer too: nothing is coming.

What you cannot take back is held, not tapped. BREAK, ending a remote console and ending stick mode fill while the finger stays down. Letting go early cancels.

Baud is a ruler. Drag it, flick it, or tap beside the marker. Auto-baud runs along the same ruler while it listens, and says so when it hears nothing.

Every gesture has a tap. Nothing can only be reached by knowing a swipe.

What it asks of the ESP32

One canvas in external RAM, as today. Sheets and dialogs are flat fills with rounded corners and a single dimming layer. No blur, no shadows.

The trace is a strip. 320 × 96 pixels, ten times a second: about 61 kB per update instead of 154 kB for a whole frame.

Motion is computed for the time that passed. A slow frame makes a bigger step, not a slower animation. Tick the box under the display to see it at 15 frames per second.

Open. A full frame takes about 86 ms on the board today. Whether a sheet can follow a finger at 15 frames per second or better has to be measured there.
